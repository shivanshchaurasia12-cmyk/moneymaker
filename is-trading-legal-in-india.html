Is Trading Legal in India? A Checklist to Run Before You Deposit Money

By the Money Moves India editorial team · Published 1 October 2026 · 4 min read

People ask "is trading legal in India?" as if there were one answer. There is not, because "trading" is a bundle of very different things. Buying shares through a registered broker is one activity. Speculating on foreign currency through an app you found on social media is another. The law treats them differently.

Why a yes-or-no answer does not work

Three things decide whether something is allowed: what you are trading (shares, futures, currency, crypto), where you are trading it (a recognised exchange or an overseas website), and who is running the platform (a registered intermediary or an unknown company).

The Reserve Bank of India has said that residents may carry out permitted forex transactions through authorised channels, and it has also published warnings about unauthorised online platforms. That is a good reason to treat the phrase "available in the app store" as meaningless. An app can be easy to download and still not be a permitted way for an Indian resident to trade.

The six-step check

StepQuestion to answerWhere to look
1What exactly am I buying or selling?The product page. Write the name of the instrument down.
2Which company runs the platform?The "About" or "Legal" page. If you cannot find a company name and address, stop.
3Is that company registered with the right authority?SEBI's website for securities brokers. RBI's website for forex rules.
4Is it on a warning list?RBI publishes an alert list of unauthorised platforms. Check the current version.
5What do the current terms say about Indian residents?The platform's own terms and risk disclosure.
6Can I get my money out?Withdrawal rules and fees, before depositing anything.

What about students and minors?

Age rules matter. SEBI has published guidance on accounts for minors: a demat account can exist in a minor's name and be operated by a guardian, but a minor cannot simply open and run a trading account alone. Do not enter a false age or use someone else's identity to get around KYC. If you are under 18, a better use of your time is to learn the basics and practise on paper while a parent or guardian handles any real account.

Red flags that should end the conversation

  • You are asked to send money to a personal bank account or UPI ID instead of a company account.
  • Someone promises fixed or "guaranteed" returns.
  • You are told to deposit quickly because a "special window" is closing.
  • The platform has no company name, no address and no support that answers questions.

Rules change, so treat any article about regulation, including this one, as a starting point. Confirm with the regulator before you act. If real money is involved and you are unsure, a registered financial adviser can give advice that fits your situation. We cannot.

Example: checking a platform you saw in an Instagram ad

Suppose an advert promises "trade global currencies from your phone, start with Rs 500". Here is how the six steps would look in practice.

  1. Product: the app offers leveraged currency contracts. Write that down. It is not the same as buying shares.
  2. Company: the website lists no company name. You find a name in the app store listing, registered in another country. That is a flag, not an answer.
  3. Regulator: the site says "regulated" but not by whom. A claim of regulation means little unless you can name the authority, the country, and confirm that it covers Indian residents.
  4. Warning list: you search RBI's site for its alert list and look for the platform name.
  5. Terms: you read whether the platform accepts customers from India at all.
  6. Withdrawals: you look for stated fees and time limits. Nothing is listed.

Three missing answers are enough reason to walk away. You lose nothing by skipping the platform, and you could lose everything by trusting it.

Questions readers ask

Is "regulated" on a website enough?

No. Ask: regulated by whom, in which country, for which product, and for customers from India? A licence in one country does not automatically allow a firm to serve people in another.

What about buying foreign shares from India?

Sending money abroad is governed by RBI rules, including the Liberalised Remittance Scheme. Limits, conditions and tax treatment can change, so read the current RBI and Income Tax Department guidance before you transfer anything.

What about crypto-assets?

The rules and the tax treatment have changed in recent years and may change again. Look for the latest official guidance and speak to a tax professional before you act.

Can a friend or parent trade for me?

Accounts must be in the name of the person who owns the money and passes KYC. Running trades through someone else's account to avoid an age or identity check causes problems for both of you.

Where to verify this

Rules, fees and menus change. Check the latest version at:

  • SEBI (sebi.gov.in) for registered brokers, stock exchange rules and the rules about minors.
  • RBI (rbi.org.in) for forex rules and its published list of unauthorised trading platforms.
  • The official terms of the platform you plan to use.
