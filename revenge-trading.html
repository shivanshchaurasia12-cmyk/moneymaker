Revenge Trading: How One Loss Turns Into Four

By the Money Moves India editorial team · Published 1 October 2026 · 4 min read

Revenge trading means taking a bigger or faster trade right after a loss, because you want the money back now. The market is not out to get you. The urge comes from the feeling that a loss is something you must undo immediately.

How it usually unfolds

The pattern is easy to spot once you have seen it a few times: a loss, then annoyance, then a larger trade with a weaker reason, then a second loss, then an even larger trade. Each step feels reasonable on its own. Together they are a spiral.

The numbers

Imagine two traders. Both start with Rs 50,000 and both lose four trades in a row. The first follows a rule to risk 1% (Rs 500) per trade. The second doubles the risk after every loss to get back faster. These figures are illustrative.

After tradeTrader A (fixed risk)Trader B (doubles risk)
StartRs 50,000Rs 50,000
Loss 1Rs 49,500 (lost 500)Rs 49,500 (lost 500)
Loss 2Rs 49,000 (lost 500)Rs 48,500 (lost 1,000)
Loss 3Rs 48,500 (lost 500)Rs 46,500 (lost 2,000)
Loss 4Rs 48,000 (lost 500)Rs 42,500 (lost 4,000)

Same market, same four losing trades. Trader A is down 4%. Trader B is down 15%. And to get back to Rs 50,000, Trader B now needs a gain of about 17.6% on what is left, while Trader A needs about 4.2%. Losses are lopsided: the deeper the hole, the larger the climb.

Warning signs in your own behaviour

  • You catch yourself saying "I just need to make it back".
  • Your position size grows without a written reason.
  • You move or remove a stop-loss after the trade has gone against you.
  • You open a new trade within minutes of closing a losing one.
  • You trade a market you have not studied because it is moving.

A cooling-off rule that works on paper

Decide this when you are calm, not in the middle of a loss. Write it where you can see it.

  1. After two losses in a row, or one loss bigger than your risk limit, stop for the day.
  2. Close the chart. Walk away for at least thirty minutes.
  3. Open your journal and write what happened: entry, exit, size, and whether you followed your plan.
  4. Next session, return to your normal position size. Never a bigger one.

Judge the process, not the result

A trade that follows your plan and loses is a normal cost of doing business. A trade that breaks your plan and wins is a bad habit that got lucky. If you reward yourself for the second kind, the habit stays. The goal is not to remove emotion. It is to stop emotion from rewriting your risk rules while money is on the line.

Why the urge is so strong

Behavioural economists have long observed that people feel a loss more sharply than they feel an equal gain. This is often called loss aversion. After a loss, the brain looks for a quick way to feel normal again, and "just one more trade" looks like that way out. Knowing the reason does not make the urge disappear. It does make it easier to recognise, and recognising it is the first step to not acting on it.

A simple journal you can copy

A journal turns "I feel like I traded badly" into something you can check. Keep it in a notebook or a spreadsheet and fill in one row per trade.

After a month, count the rows where you answered "no" to following the plan. Compare the total result of those trades with the ones where you followed it. Many people find that the rule-breaking trades are where most of the damage sits.

If you are already in a spiral

Close the position that broke your rules if it is still open, step away from the screen, and tell one person what happened. It sounds small, but saying it aloud removes some of the shame that fuels the next impulsive trade.
